Mandarin Hero's lessons start with **real conversations**, phrased the way people actually talk, from the very first lesson. That makes the start harder: more new words, faster phrasing, more than one way to say the same thing. We chose that on purpose. **We'd rather you struggle during practice than freeze when it matters.**

Here's why, what it costs you, and how the app keeps the hurdle climbable.

## The problem with easy dialogues

Most of us met the same first dialogue in some form:

> **你好吗？** Nǐ hǎo ma? — How are you?
> **我很好，谢谢。你呢？** Wǒ hěn hǎo, xièxie. Nǐ ne? — I'm very well, thank you. And you?

It's tidy, every word is easy, and you feel like you're making progress. But it's not how people greet each other in everyday life, and nobody at a shop, a bubble tea counter or a taxi rank will say anything like it to you.

That's the trap of a dumbed-down dialogue. **It gives you confidence for a conversation that never happens.** You find out at the very end, when you're finally standing at the counter, someone says something fast and natural, and none of it sounds like what you practised. That moment is the worst possible time to learn that your training didn't match the real thing.

## What real Mandarin sounds like

In real life, people don't stick to one script. In Mandarin Hero's very first lesson, *First words*, the shopkeeper can open the role-play in any of these ways:

> **你好！要什么？** Nǐ hǎo! Yào shénme? — Hi! What would you like?
> **你好，你要什么？** Nǐ hǎo, nǐ yào shénme? — Hi, what are you after?
> **要什么？** Yào shénme? — What would you like?
> **你好，看看？** Nǐ hǎo, kànkan? — Hi, having a look?

Same moment, four different sentences. In the *Bubble tea* lesson, the person behind the counter might say **你好，要喝点什么？** (Nǐ hǎo, yào hē diǎn shénme?, "Hi, what can I get you?"), or just a quick **来了，喝点啥？** (Lái le, hē diǎn shá?, "Hi there, what'll it be?"), with the casual **啥** instead of the textbook **什么**.

If you've only ever heard one tidy version, any of the others can throw you. If you've heard all of them in practice, you just answer.

## Fail in training, not in real life

The idea is simple: **practise under the same conditions you'll face for real.** Memory researchers call it *transfer-appropriate processing*: you remember best when the way you practised matches the way you need the knowledge later ([Morris, Bransford & Franks, 1977](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9)). If you practise slow, simplified Mandarin, you get good at slow, simplified Mandarin.

It also fits how memory works in general. Effort and mistakes during learning, followed by the right answer, make things stick better than an easy ride. We wrote about that in [guess, struggle, remember](/apps/mandarinhero/blog/best-way-to-memorize-vocabulary/). A conversation you struggled with in practice is one you'll recognise when it comes at you for real.

| | Simplified dialogues | Real conversations from day one |
|---|---|---|
| The first lessons | Easy, quick wins | Harder, more new words |
| How it feels at first | Confident | Sometimes overwhelmed |
| Your first real conversation | A shock: it sounds nothing like class | Familiar: you've heard it before |
| Where you make your mistakes | At the counter, in front of people | In the app, where nobody's watching |

## What it costs (we won't pretend otherwise)

This choice has a price, and you pay it at the start.

- **A bigger vocabulary wall.** A real conversation needs more words than a made-up one. *First words* already has 27 key words, *Numbers* 47, and *Bubble tea* 65.
- **Less instant satisfaction.** You won't sail through your first lesson. Some lines will feel too fast or too long.
- **It can feel discouraging.** Especially in the first week, before the common words start coming back by themselves.

We think that's worth it, because the alternative costs more later: weeks of lessons that turn out not to help when you actually need them.

## How the app keeps the wall climbable

Hard on purpose doesn't mean hard for the sake of it. Each lesson is built so the conversation is the *last* step, not the first.

1. **Key words first.** Every lesson starts with its key words as cards, with audio and a breakdown of what each character means.
2. **Then the handy phrases**, with a word-by-word breakdown, so you see how each sentence is put together.
3. **Then the conversation**, which unlocks once you've learned both. By then, most of what you hear is something you've already met.
4. **A role-play where you say your part out loud**, with a hint for what to say, and several right answers, because there's more than one way to say it.
5. **A "Huh?" button**, which has the other side say it another way, just like a real person would if you looked lost.
6. **Practice** brings back the words you missed straight away, and everything else on a spaced schedule, until every card for a word reaches a 21-day interval.

And the very first lesson, *First words*, is the glue: **我** (wǒ, I), **你** (nǐ, you), **这个** (zhège, this one), **要** (yào, to want), **吗** (ma, the question word). With those you can already point at something and say:

> **我要这个。** Wǒ yào zhège. — I'll have this one.

That's a real sentence you can use in a real shop on your first day.

## The bottom line

Real Mandarin is messy, fast and full of variety. You'll meet it eventually. The only question is where: in a practice session, where you can hear it again and again, or at a counter with a queue behind you. We'd rather it's the first.
