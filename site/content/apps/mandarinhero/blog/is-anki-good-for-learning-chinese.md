**Anki is one of the best tools there is for remembering Chinese words.** It's built on the two study techniques with the strongest evidence behind them, and plenty of learners have used it to build huge vocabularies. But many of them run into the same wall: **they know thousands of words and still freeze in a conversation.** Anki fills your head with words. It doesn't teach you to use them.

Here's what Anki does well, what real learners found, and what closes the gap.

## What Anki does well

Anki shows you one side of a card, you try to recall the other side, and you rate how well you did. Words you know come back after longer and longer gaps. Words you forget come back soon.

That's **retrieval practice** plus **spacing**, the two techniques a large review of learning research rated most useful. We wrote about the studies behind them in [guess, struggle, remember](/apps/mandarinhero/blog/best-way-to-memorize-vocabulary/).

A few more things in its favour:

- **It's free** on desktop, Android and the web. The iPhone app is paid.
- **It keeps getting better.** Since version 23.10 (October 2023), Anki can schedule with [FSRS](https://docs.ankiweb.net/faqs/what-spaced-repetition-algorithm), a newer algorithm that times reviews more precisely than the old one.
- **It works at scale.** Medical students are among Anki's heaviest users. In one survey of 201 students, Anki users scored higher on the US medical licensing exam, 241 against 236 on average ([Lu et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40670-021-01386-9)). That's a survey, not an experiment, but it fits what memory research predicts.

## Learners who made it work

**Olle Linge**, who runs Hacking Chinese, one of the best-known sites for Chinese learners, has written that he has studied over **20,000 Chinese words**, would expect to score 90–95% on a test of all of them, and spends about **30 minutes a day** keeping them fresh ([as republished by CLI](https://studycli.org/learn-chinese/3-helpful-learning-techniques-from-hacking-chinese/)).

On the Chinese Forums, a learner studying full-time in China described Anki like this ([David Ma, 2015](https://www.chinese-forums.com/forums/topic/49949-my-2-year-journey-to-mandarin-native-pronunciation-part-4-how-i-use-anki/)):

> "The upside is that it lets you cram a lot of new words into your long-term memory with minimal effort."

He also said making his own cards was what really helped, and admitted it's not very interesting.

That matters, because **vocabulary is where Chinese takes the most time.** For an English or Dutch speaker, almost no Chinese word looks or sounds familiar. Each one comes with a tone, often a new character, and nothing to hang it on. The US Foreign Service Institute puts Mandarin among the hardest languages for English speakers, at around 2,200 class hours. A tool that makes words stick is a big deal.

## Where it falls short: words aren't conversations

Here's the catch. In 2015, a learner on the same forum wrote that they had just passed **HSK 4 with 80%**, had three tutors, and still had an active vocabulary of about 200 words when speaking ([Walkingtree, 2015](https://www.chinese-forums.com/forums/topic/47550-how-to-move-vocabulary-into-the-active-zone/)):

> "I feel like a child when it comes to be my turn to speak."

It's a common story, and Olle Linge himself explains why. A flashcard schedule is designed to bring a word back just before you forget it. That's efficient, but ["just barely recalling what something means is not enough"](https://www.hackingchinese.com/when-spaced-repetition-fails-and-what-to-do-about-it/):

> "If you stop and think about every word, you will not be able to have a conversation."

Picture it. You know **咖啡** (kāfēi, coffee), **热** (rè, hot) and **杯** (bēi, cup). Then you walk into a café, and this is what the person behind the counter says, from Mandarin Hero's *Café* lesson:

> **你好，喝点什么？** Nǐ hǎo, hē diǎn shénme? — Hi, what can I get you?
> **大杯还是中杯？** Dà bēi háishi zhōng bēi? — Large or medium?
> **在这儿喝还是带走？** Zài zhèr hē háishi dàizǒu? — For here or to go?
> **一共三十一，扫这儿。** Yígòng sānshíyī, sǎo zhèr. — Thirty-one, scan here.

Every word is simple. But it comes fast, in full sentences, with no card in front of you and a queue behind you. Knowing the words on a card is one skill. **Catching them in real speech and answering right away is another**, and Anki doesn't train it.

## Anki covers one strand out of four

Language researcher Paul Nation describes a balanced course as four strands, each getting roughly a quarter of your time. He suggests **no more than about 25%** goes to studying words and grammar directly (summed up well [on Sinosplice](https://www.sinosplice.com/life/archives/2019/06/12/the-four-strands-of-a-balanced-language-course)).

| Strand | What it means | Does Anki do it? |
|---|---|---|
| Language-focused learning | Studying words, tones, grammar on purpose | **Yes**, very well |
| Meaning-focused input | Listening to (and reading) real Chinese | No |
| Meaning-focused output | Saying what you mean | No |
| Fluency | Using what you already know until it's fast | No |

That's why someone can spend a year in Anki and still not hold a conversation. They've done one strand very well, and skipped the other three.

The fix, as one forum member put it: **"The answer is essentially to use it."** Put new words into sentences, hear them in real conversations, and say them out loud until they come without thinking.

## The bottom line

Anki works. It's one of the most efficient ways to get Chinese words into long-term memory, and vocabulary is the slowest part of learning Chinese. But words alone don't make you speak. **You need to hear them in real conversations and say them out loud**, again and again, until they come without thinking.
